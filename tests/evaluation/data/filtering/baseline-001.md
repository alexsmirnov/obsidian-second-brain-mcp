## Abstract
Since the COVID-19 pandemic, most general practices in England have adopted online consultation (OC) systems for easier access and demand management. However, there are concerns that easier access could increase demand relating to low-risk minor illness, with consequential changes in prescribing patterns. Using GP practice-level data we investigate effects of adoption of an OC system in English general practice on antibiotic prescribing rates. The target estimand is the average effect of adoption on the outcome in each month post-adoption within groups of practices defined by month of adoption. Estimation is using a doubly robust staggered difference-in-differences approach. We also estimate an effect aggregated across all adopting practices and across time since adoption. Overall, we find evidence that adoption of an OC system results in higher antibiotic prescribing rates, with our primary analysis indicating an overall 5.1% increase. However, using recently proposed methods, we find that our estimates are sensitive to possible violations of the key identification assumption, conditional parallel trends. When allowing for relatively small violations of this assumption, we would no longer conclude that there is statistically significant evidence of an effect and smaller in magnitude effects become more plausible, which may be considered reassuring for health policy.

## 1 Introduction
The use of digital tools in health care settings is growing and has been accelerated by the COVID-19 pandemic. In the UK, there has been increased adoption of online consultation (OC) systems by general practices. These systems enable patients to contact their general practice by submitting an online form with details of the query ([Eccles et al., 2019](javascript:;)). After this initial contact, patients may be offered face-to-face appointments, or queries may be dealt with remotely by telephone, video, or via online message.
Adoption of an OC system aims to improve the experience of patients by being convenient and accessible, and of practitioners through increasing efficiency of their practice ([Eccles et al., 2019](javascript:;)). However, it is important to investigate whether adoption could have any unintended implications. Antibiotic prescribing is of particular concern as the misuse and overuse of antibiotics accelerates antibiotic resistance ([World Health Organisation, 2020](javascript:;)). Since the majority of antibiotics in England are prescribed within general practice ([UK Health Security Agency, 2022](javascript:;)), this motivates assessing whether adoption of an OC system causes a change in antibiotic prescribing. In this study, we investigate the impact of adoption of an OC system on antibiotic prescribing rates using practice-level data.
There are several potential mechanisms through which adoption of an OC system could result in changes in antibiotic prescribing rates. It has been suggested that if ease of access is improved by adoption, there could be a lower ‘threshold’ at which patients decide to contact their practice, potentially uncovering previously unmet need or generating new patient demand ([Salisbury, 2021](javascript:;)). It has been hypothesized that higher rates of patient-initiated demand relating to low risk minor illness could lead to an increase in antibiotic prescribing rates. Adoption could also result in a shift in the type of patients making contact, for example increasing contact from patients who find verbal communication difficult and potentially have differing care requirements ([Turner et al., 2022](javascript:;)). Another potential mechanism could relate to practitioner’s work pressure, since this may increase with improved patient access ([Newbould et al., 2017](javascript:;)) and has been associated with increased prescribing of antibiotics ([Allen et al., 2022](javascript:;)). Alternatively, if adoption of an OC system impacts patients’ choice of the GP practice they are registered to, prescribing rates could potentially be affected by changes in practice list sizes. Patients may be attracted to improved ease of access after a practice has adopted for instance. However, we do not believe adoption would lead to a considerable shift in patient registrations. Prescribing of antibiotics is often necessary and any increase in rates under adoption of an OC system could reflect higher quality care. However, it is crucial that antibiotics are appropriately prescribed, irrespective of patient demand or expectation. Therefore, it is important to establish whether adoption of an OC system causes a change in antibiotic prescribing rates, which could then inform further research to explore potential mechanisms for any change and the appropriateness of prescribing.
There are 31 OC systems that are approved for use by the NHS, varying in design, functionality, and how they are implemented ([Chappell et al., 2023](javascript:;)). We focus on the _askmyGP_ OC system version 3, hereafter referred to as _askmyGP_ (<https://askmygp.uk/>). Practices using _askmyGP_ are encouraged to use the system to facilitate ‘total triage’. Under this model, all patient contact to the practice is channelled through an OC system, which patients can do themselves or with assistance from administrative staff, creating a single workflow to support the triage of patient contacts ([NHS England, 2020](javascript:;)). In May 2023, NHS England committed to supporting all general practices to implement a total triage model, which they refer to as ‘Modern General Practice Access’ ([NHS England, 2023](javascript:;)). Since _askmyGP_ facilitates this model being promoted by the NHS, the impacts of its adoption are particularly relevant.
As with many health policy interventions, _askmyGP_ has been adopted at a cluster level, with different general practices adopting at different times. When evaluating the effects of adoption using practice-level data available across a series of time periods, consideration is required as to how to make use of outcomes measured for a general practice before and after adoption and how to separate the effects of the intervention from other time trends.
The focus is on estimating the effect of adopting the OC system in those practices that adopted it, that is an average treatment effect in the treated (ATT). When all treated units initiate treatment at the same time, the two-way fixed effects difference-in-differences (DiD) estimator is a commonly used approach for estimating the ATT, and provides an unbiased estimate under identification assumptions such as the parallel trends assumption ([de Chaisemartin & D’Haultfœuille, 2022](javascript:;)). However, when roll-out of an intervention is staggered over time, recent literature has shown that this estimator can be biased for the ATT when there is treatment effect heterogeneity over time since treatment initiation ([de Chaisemartin & D'Haultfœuille, 2020](javascript:;); [Goodman-Bacon, 2021](javascript:;)). In extreme cases, the ATT can even be negative when treatment effects are positive for all units ([Borusyak et al., 2022](javascript:;)). Several methods have since been proposed to handle staggered roll-out of interventions ([Borusyak et al., 2022](javascript:;); [Callaway & Sant’Anna, 2021](javascript:;); [de Chaisemartin & D'Haultfœuille, 2020](javascript:;); [Sun & Abraham, 2021](javascript:;); [Wooldridge, 2021](javascript:;)). [Roth et al. (2023)](javascript:;) compared these recent advancements in detail ([Roth et al., 2023](javascript:;) ). They highlight that the estimators differ in which units they use when forming the comparison group and in the pre-treatment time periods used to estimate differences over time. This leads to trade-offs between efficiency and the strength of the corresponding parallel trends assumption needed for identification. However, they found that the various methods typically produce similar results.
In this study, we consider one such method proposed by [Callaway and Sant’Anna (2021)](javascript:;). This has been shown to be preferable to [Borusyak et al. (2022)](javascript:;) when there are concerns about the validity of the parallel trends assumption over longer time periods ([Roth et al., 2023](javascript:;)). The approach estimates ATTs for groups of treated units defined by the time period of treatment initiation on outcomes measured in a series of post-treatment time periods. These ATTs are referred to as group-time average treatment effects (GTATTs). We refer to this estimation approach as the staggered DiD approach throughout.
Our study is the first to use the staggered DiD approach to investigate effects of adoption of OC systems, though it has been used to assess some implications of the use of telemedicine more broadly ([Dong, 2022](javascript:;)). A previous study of the impact of adoption of _askmyGP_ on antibiotic prescribing rates has been undertaken, but the analysis did not consider the staggered adoption of the system ([Dias & Clarke, 2020](javascript:;)).

## 2 Data sources
### 2.1 Defining the intervention, control group, and analysis period
The aim is to investigate the impact of adoption of _askmyGP_ on antibiotic prescribing rates among general practices that adopted the system between March 2019 and February 2020. These practices adopted _askmyGP_ prior to widespread adoption of OC systems during the COVID-19 pandemic and are referred to as the ‘early adopters’ throughout. We make use of monthly practice-level data on adoption of _askmyGP_ (yes or no), antibiotic prescribing rates, and practice characteristics. Our approach requires data on outcome and practice characteristics in months prior to adoption of _askmyGP_ for adopting practices, and on general practices that did not use an OC system in any month under analysis.
We obtained a list of unique identifiers of general practices that used _askmyGP_ at any point after its launch in July 2018, up until March 2022. We define the month of adoption of _askmyGP_ as the first month in which there was at least one patient-initiated contact recorded through the system. We assume that once a practice first adopted the system, it continued to use it for the rest of our analysis period.
Since we did not have access to data on adoption of other OC systems, identifying suitable control practices that that did not use an OC system in any month under analysis was challenging. In March 2020, in response to the COVID-19 pandemic, there was strong guidance from the NHS that all practices must have access to an OC system to support triage ([NHS England, 2020](javascript:;)). We made the assumption that practices that adopted _askmyGP_ from March 2020 adopted an OC system in response to this guidance and had not used another OC system prior to this. We refer to these practices, which adopted _askmyGP_ between March 2020 and March 2022, as the ‘late adopters’, and they form our control group, with all effects assessed over the period preceding their adoption of _askmyGP_. We only make use of data from practices defined as either early adopters or late adopters, while meeting certain other inclusion criteria (see of the for details).
We define our analysis period, over which binary treatment status, outcomes, and covariates are available, as May 2017 to February 2020. The 22-month period from May 2017 to February 2019 is defined as the pre-adoption time period and the 12-month period from March 2019 to February 2020 as the post-adoption period.
### 2.2 Outcome
The outcome of interest is the monthly antibiotic prescribing rates from each month in the whole analysis period (May 2017–February 2020) on both the early and late adopters. The prescribing rate is defined as the total number of items of antibacterial drugs prescribed each month divided by the monthly practice list size (the number of patients registered to the practice). Antibacterial drugs are classified as such by the British National Formulary ([OpenPrescribing, 2023a](javascript:;)). This data is publicly available from OpenPrescribing, a search interface of the raw English Prescribing Dataset published by the NHS Business Services Authority ([OpenPrescribing, 2023b](javascript:;)). The monthly practice list size was obtained from publicly available data ([NHS Digital, 2022](javascript:;)).
### 2.3 Covariates
For each included general practice, each month, we estimated the percentage of registered patients that were male ([Office for National Statistics, 2021](javascript:;)), aged 65 years and over ([Office for National Statistics, 2021](javascript:;)), and of black and minority ethnicity ([Office for National Statistics, 2022](javascript:;)); the index of multiple deprivation score of patient area ([GOV.UK, 2019](javascript:;)); and a binary indicator for the classification of rurality of patient area ([Office for National Statistics, 2022](javascript:;)). These sources produce metrics at Lower Layer Super Output Area (LSOA) level, which we then aggregated to practice-level using publicly available data on the number of patients registered to each English general practice that live in each LSOA ([NHS Digital, 2022](javascript:;)) (see of the for details).

### 4.2 Application of the staggered difference-in-differences approach
We applied the doubly robust staggered DiD approach to each group _g_ of early adopters (defined by month of adoption ), using the late adopters (who adopted the system after the end of our analysis period and so are ‘never treated’) as the control group. We carried out the analysis at the monthly level, between May 2017 and February 2020 (). Our covariate specification included the estimates of the percentage of male patients, percentage of patients aged 65 years and over, percentage of patients of black and minority ethnicity, index of multiple deprivation score of patient area, and binary indicator for the classification of rurality of patient area. As recommended by [Callaway and Sant’Anna (2021)](javascript:;) (see Remark 12), we specified a varying base period. This is where the comparator time period is for pre-treatment GTATTs, rather than as used for post-treatment GTATTs. The analysis was performed using the _csdid_ package in Stata ([Rios-Avila, 2023](javascript:;)). To give a better indication of the magnitude of effect, we also estimated the overall expected percentage increase in average prescribing rates post-adoption of the system, compared to if the early adopters had not adopted it. We estimated this percentage by subtracting the overall ATT estimate ( in (4)) from the observed mean prescribing rate for the early adopters between March 2019 and February 2020, and then divided the overall ATT estimate by this difference (which is the estimated mean counterfactual outcome for the early adopters between March 2019 and February 2020).
The estimated GTATTs ( in (1)) for each group of early adopters in each post-adoption month are given in see . The 78 post-adoption GTATT estimates, corresponding to the 78 post-adoption combinations of _g_ and _t_ , vary and give correspondingly varied evidence of an association between adoption of the system and antibiotic prescribing rates. Assuming the identification assumptions hold, the GTATTs for the first group of early adopters () all give strong evidence of a positive association between adoption and antibiotic prescribing rates, and the effect estimates generally increase with time since adoption. However, many GTATTs for other groups give little evidence of an effect.
The estimated overall average effect of adoption of the system experienced by the early adopters ( in (4)) was 2.3—this is the expected increase in the monthly numbers of items of antibiotics prescribed per 1,000 patients post-adoption of the system, compared to if the early adopters had not adopted it. Assuming the identification assumptions hold, there is strong evidence of an effect of adoption when averaged across all the early adopters [2.3 items per 1,000 patients, 95% CI = (0.5, 4.0), _p_ -value = .013]. This overall ATT translates into an estimated 5.1% increase in antibiotic prescribing rates post-adoption of the system, compared to if these practices had not adopted it.
[Table 2](javascript:;) gives the estimates of the average effect of adoption for each group of early adopters ( in (2)). These average group effect estimates vary considerably, suggesting that adoption of the system has a heterogeneous effect across groups of practices that adopted it at different times. However, there does not appear to be a trend in the magnitude of effect according to time of adoption. [Figure 3](javascript:;) gives the estimates of the average effect of having adopted a particular number of months ago ( in (3)). These average exposure effect estimates also vary, suggesting that adoption of the system has a heterogeneous effect according to time since adoption. With increasing time since adoption, there appears to be a somewhat positive trend in the magnitude of effect. The estimated average effect of having adopted 11 months ago is considerably larger than for other months. Although this step change in magnitude of effect does not seem plausible, this is only one estimated GTATT for group who experienced a large effect of adoption in all post-adoption periods. Since this group is comprised of only one practice, this does not substantially contribute to the overall estimate, as it is weighted by group size (the number of practices). Excluding this GP practice from the main analysis results in an estimated overall average effect of adoption of 2.0 [95% CI = (0.1, 3.8)] compared to 2.3 [95% CI = (0.5, 4.0)].

To assess sensitivity of estimates displayed in [Figure 3](javascript:;) to possible violations of the conditional parallel trends assumption, we carried out [Rambachan and Roth’s (2023)](javascript:;) approach ([Rambachan & Roth, 2023](javascript:;)) as described above. Their approach allows for violations of the conditional parallel trends assumption extrapolated from pre-treatment periods. See for the results of our smoothness restriction sensitivity analysis, reporting the breakdown value and corresponding confidence interval at the breakdown value for estimates that were still statistically significant when allowing for linear violations of parallel trends (). These confidence intervals give the range of effects that would just about be plausible under the imposed non-linear violations of conditional parallel trends. The largest breakdown value across all post-adoption months is relatively low at 0.028, indicating that our findings of significant effects are sensitive to the conditional parallel tends assumption holding. If the true post-adoption difference in trends between treated and control groups deviates from the linear pre-trend by this amount or more, we would not obtain a statistically significant effect of adoption of the OC system at any month post-adoption. However, bias is likely to overstate rather than understate any effects of adoption of an OC system; the 95% CI under linear non-parallel trends (Column 3) for each exposure duration typically implies lower effect estimates than the 95% CI from our original analysis assuming parallel trends (Column 2). The confidence intervals shift towards zero because there is a positive pre-adoption trend ([Figure 3](javascript:;)) so accounting for this reduces the estimated effect. For our bounds on relative magnitudes sensitivity analysis, we impose that the post-treatment violation of parallel trends is no more than some constant times the maximum violation of parallel trends between consecutive pre-treatment periods. Again, we find that our findings of significant effects are sensitive to the conditional parallel trends assumption holding, with all breakdown values ≤0.057.
Although we do not give details here, we also carried out a sensitivity analysis using 6,221 English general practices that did not adopt _askmyGP_ in any period as the comparators (rather than the 143 late adopters of _askmyGP_) and a longer analysis period (up until February 2022) and came to similar conclusions.
## 5 Discussion
We used a recently proposed staggered DiD approach ([Callaway & Sant’Anna, 2021](javascript:;)) to investigate the impact of adoption of an OC system in general practices in England on antibiotic prescribing rates, using longitudinal data from 182 general practices. We assessed sensitivity to possible violations of our main identification assumption using recently proposed methods ([Rambachan & Roth, 2023](javascript:;)).
Our results using Callaway and Sant’Anna’s ([2021](javascript:;)) approach suggest that adoption of _askmyGP_ tends to increase antibiotic prescribing rates. In 2016, NHS England launched a national programme to combat antibiotic resistance where clinical commissioning groups were supported to reduce the number of antibiotics prescribed in primary care by 4% ([NHS England, 2016](javascript:;)). This suggests that even small changes in antibiotic prescribing rates are considered important and an overall estimated increase of 5.1% post-adoption of the system under our main analysis may be non-trivial. However, this conclusion relies on the identification assumptions holding. By allowing for some violation of the conditional parallel trends assumption extrapolated from pre-treatment periods we would no longer conclude that there is evidence of an effect. Notably, when allowing for some violation, our confidence intervals typically cover values smaller in magnitude than under our main analysis. This is due to the positive pre-adoption trend ([Figure 3](javascript:;)) accounting for part of the original estimated effect. This indicates that adoption of an OC system is unlikely to have significant unintended consequences on antibiotic prescribing rates. This may be considered reassuring for health policy since adoption of an OC system is increasingly common in English general practice.
There is evidence of wide variation in the effect of adoption for different groups of practices that adopted the system at different times. This seems credible as effects would likely vary according to practice and patient characteristics. Moreover, it is likely that practice staff and patients utilized the system in different ways, leading to varying effects. For instance, some practices might have hardly advertised the system to their patients over the post-adoption months that we analysed. There is also some suggestion that the magnitude of effect may tend to increase with increasing time since adoption.
The doubly robust method applied to the staggered DiD approach considered in this article conditions on measured pre-treatment covariates measured in the period prior to adoption and relies on a conditional parallel trends assumption. Taking a conditional DiD approach is necessary when there are differences in measured pre-treatment characteristics that create non-parallel outcome trends between groups in the absence of treatment initiation ([Callaway & Sant’Anna, 2021](javascript:;)). In our application, we condition on the values of practice-level characteristics in the time period just prior to adoption of the system; we do not account for the time-varying nature of these characteristics. The presence of time-varying characteristics associated with both adoption of the system and antibiotic prescribing rates may violate our conditional parallel trends assumption and cause confounding bias. [Zeldow and Hatfield (2021)](javascript:;) discuss time-varying confounding in DiD designs in detail ([Zeldow & Hatfield, 2021](javascript:;)). They note that time-varying covariates associated with both treatment initiation and the outcome are confounders in DiD designs if they have a time-varying difference between treatment groups or if they evolve over time and have a time-varying effect on the outcome. Moreover, since the pre-adoption practice-level characteristics used in our analyses were only estimates of the characteristics of patients registered to each practice, there may have been some residual confounding.
One potential limitation of our study is that we did not make use of data on levels of usage of the system. However, our analyses can be considered as intention to treat analyses since we included practices irrespective of their level of usage or whether they potentially stopped using the system. Another limitation of our study is that we had a relatively short follow-up time. This was to ensure that there were general practices that did not use an OC system during the analysis period to use as comparators. Therefore, we cannot determine the longer-term effects of adoption. It seems plausible that effects could increase as patients become more accustomed to the system and utilize it more frequently.
Another limitation of our study relates to the definition of our control group. We aimed to estimate the effect of adoption of _askmyGP_ , compared to not adopting any OC system. It seems plausible that the late adopters of _askmyGP_ adopted an OC system from March 2020 in response to guidance from the NHS ([NHS England, 2020](javascript:;)). In which case, they would not have been using an OC system during our analysis period. However, since we did not have access to data on adoption of other OC systems, we cannot confirm this. Our sensitivity analysis using all non-adopters of _askmyGP_ (6,221 practices), rather than the 143 late adopters of _askmyGP_ , produced similar results.
A previous evaluation on the effects of implementation of total triage, through adoption of _askmyGP,_ on antibiotic prescribing rates used the synthetic control method ([Dias & Clarke, 2020](javascript:;)). While they found a slight increase in average antibiotic prescribing rates, they found no evidence of an effect of implementation of total triage during 2019 for 19 English practices that adopted _askmyGP_ between August 2018 and mid-2019 (ATT = 0.61 items per 1,000 patients per month, 95% CI = [−2.2, 4.9], _p_ -value = .534). Since they considered a different group of practices, that were all using _askmyGP_ to facilitate total triage, their findings are not directly comparable to ours. The synthetic control method relies on different assumptions to the staggered DiD approach considered in this paper. In particular, it does not rely on the conditional parallel trends assumption, but on there being a close pre-treatment match between the characteristics of the treatment group and of a weighted combination of control units ([Rehkopf & Basu, 2018](javascript:;)). Similarly to DiD methods, extensions have been proposed that handle variation in timing of treatment initiation, such as the generalized synthetic control method ([Xu, 2017](javascript:;)).
Although adoption of an OC system is unlikely to have significant unintended consequences on antibiotic prescribing rates, our results reinforce the importance of practitioners adhering to prescribing guidelines, and the public being educated on responsible use of antibiotics, to ensure that any potential increase in rates under adoption of the system is appropriate. Prescribing of antibiotics is often necessary and any increase in rates could reflect higher quality care. However, it is crucial that antibiotics are appropriately prescribed, irrespective of patient demand or expectation. This is currently particularly relevant as there will potentially be an increase in adoption of _askmyGP_ as practices are being supported by the NHS to implement total triage ([NHS England, 2023](javascript:;)). Since we solely investigated the impact of adoption of _askmyGP_ , it is difficult to determine how generalizable our results may be to other OC systems as they all vary in functionality. Further research is needed to assess potential impacts of adoption of OC systems and alternative statistical methods, such as the generalized synthetic control method ([Xu, 2017](javascript:;)), should be applied to assess the validity of our results.
